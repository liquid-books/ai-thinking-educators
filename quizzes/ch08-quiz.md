## Short Answer (3 questions)

**Question 1** (suggested response length: 150–200 words):

Describe Mezirow's ten phases of transformative learning and explain how the arrival of AI in K-12 classrooms could be understood as a disorienting dilemma for teachers. At what phase do you think most K-12 teachers currently are, and why?

*Model Answer:* Mezirow's ten phases begin with a disorienting dilemma — an encounter that challenges existing assumptions — and proceed through self-examination, critical assessment of assumptions, recognition of shared discontent, exploration of new roles, planning, skill acquisition, trying new roles, building competence and confidence, and finally reintegration into practice with a transformed perspective. The arrival of AI functions as a disorienting dilemma because it disrupts established assumptions about the teacher's role as content deliverer, the value of traditional assessment forms, and the nature of student-produced work. Most K-12 teachers are currently somewhere in Phases 3–6 — past the initial shock and self-examination but still in the exploration and planning stages, not yet having built full competence in the transformed frame. Evidence: widespread policy debates, varied institutional responses, and the coexistence of teachers who have fully redesigned their practice with teachers who have dismissed AI entirely or are paralyzed by uncertainty.

---

**Question 2** (suggested response length: 150–200 words):

The chapter distinguishes between threats worth worrying about and threats that are overblown. Choose one from each category, explain why each deserves or doesn't deserve serious concern, and describe what a thoughtful teacher response would look like for each.

*Model Answer:* A real threat: cognitive dependency — students habitually using AI to bypass cognitive work rather than scaffold it. This deserves serious concern because the effects are invisible in the short term (outputs look fine) but compound over time (capacity doesn't develop). A thoughtful teacher response: deliberately design assignments where the intellectual work cannot be completed by AI without the student's active engagement — oral defenses, process documentation, revision narratives, and authentic creation tasks. An overblown threat: AI will replace teachers. This does not deserve the alarm it receives because the skills that make teachers irreplaceable — relational attunement, contextual judgment, human witness, emotional attunement to individual students — are precisely what AI cannot replicate. Framing this as the primary threat distracts from the real work of adapting professional practice.

**Question 3** (suggested response length: 150–200 words):

The chapter's "Letter to the Teacher of 2035" argues that the teachers who navigate the AI transition most successfully will be those who carry "the old craft and the new understanding together." What does this mean? What specifically from the "old craft" should be preserved, and what specifically from the new understanding needs to be built?

*Model Answer:* The old craft to preserve includes the relational skills of teaching — knowing individual students as whole people, reading the room, making judgment calls that require contextual knowledge no AI has, providing the human witness that makes students willing to take learning risks. It also includes deep content knowledge, the ability to recognize genuine understanding versus performed understanding, and the pedagogical principles grounded in decades of learning science (desirable difficulties, cognitive load, productive failure). The new understanding to build includes fluency with AI tools and their capabilities and limitations, the ability to design learning experiences that leverage AI as a scaffold rather than a bypass, skill in auditing AI outputs for accuracy and bias, and the ability to lead colleagues through the transformative learning process that AI is activating across the profession. The synthesis — not replacement of one by the other — is what the chapter calls the transformed perspective: teaching as the design of conditions for human learning, supported but not substituted by intelligent tools.
