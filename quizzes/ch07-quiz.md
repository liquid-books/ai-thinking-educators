## Short Answer (3 questions)

**11.** Explain the "curriculum has to climb" argument made in Section 7.2. What does this mean in practical terms for lesson planning, and what does it require teachers to do differently than they have in the past?

*Sample answer: The "curriculum has to climb" argument holds that because AI now performs the lower three tiers of Bloom's taxonomy (Remember, Understand, Apply) competently and cheaply, instruction and assessment that live primarily at those tiers can be bypassed by students without meaningful learning loss. Teachers must design instruction and assessment that centers the upper three tiers — Analyze, Evaluate, Create — where human cognition, judgment, and lived experience remain essential. In practical terms, this means moving away from tasks that produce content recall (define, list, summarize) toward tasks that require genuine intellectual work: evaluating competing arguments, synthesizing across sources, creating original work that requires personal knowledge and defensible judgment.*

---

**12.** Define Flavell's metacognition in your own words and explain why the chapter argues it is "the central skill" for students in the AI era. Give one concrete classroom example of a teacher building metacognitive capacity in an AI-present lesson.

*Sample answer: Metacognition (Flavell, 1979) is knowledge and control of one's own cognitive processes — the ability to plan how to approach a task, monitor one's own understanding during it, and evaluate what was actually learned afterward. In the AI era, metacognition is the central skill because the difference between AI-fluent and AI-dependent students is entirely metacognitive: fluent students use AI with intention (planning what to use it for, monitoring whether it is helping or replacing their thinking, evaluating what they actually learned), while dependent students use it without intention. Example: Before any AI-assisted activity, a teacher gives students 3 minutes to write their current best thinking on the question — no tools. After using AI, students document: "What did AI add? What did I already know? What do I actually understand now that I could explain without AI?" This closing reflection builds the monitoring and evaluating phases of Flavell's cycle.*

---

**13.** Using Fink's Taxonomy of Significant Learning, explain why the emergence of AI tools does not reduce the importance of the teacher — and may actually increase it. Reference at least two specific categories from Fink's framework in your response.

*Sample answer: Fink's taxonomy identifies six categories of significant learning. While Foundational Knowledge (the category that historically consumed most instructional time) is now AI-cheap — AI can explain, define, and demonstrate with speed and patience — the remaining categories require human presence and cannot be replicated by AI. The Human Dimension — learning about oneself and others, developing self-knowledge, recognizing one's own growth — happens through relationship, through being seen by someone who knows you, through the experience of mattering to another person. AI cannot provide this. Caring — the development of genuine values, interests, and passions — is sparked by mentors, by teachers who model what it looks like to care deeply about something, by the contagious quality of authentic intellectual engagement. A chatbot cannot be authentically passionate. As AI takes over Foundational Knowledge delivery, teachers are freed to invest more of their time and energy in exactly these irreducible categories — making the teacher's role not smaller but more concentrated in the work that most powerfully shapes human development.*
